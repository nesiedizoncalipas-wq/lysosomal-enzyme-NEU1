## 2. Selected Enzyme

- **Gene symbol:** NEU1
- **Enzyme name:** Neuraminidase 1 (sialidase-1 precursor)
- **Organism:** *Homo sapiens* (human)
- **NCBI protein accession:** NP_000425.1
- **Protein length:** 415 amino acids
- **NCBI protein record:** [NP_000425.1 – Sialidase-1 precursor](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/protein/NP_000425.1)

### Normal Function

NEU1 is a lysosomal enzyme that removes terminal sialic acid residues from molecules such as glycoproteins and glycolipids. This activity helps cells break down and recycle these substances. NEU1 also works as part of a protein complex with beta-galactosidase and cathepsin A.

## 5. Methods

### Step 1: Finding the protein sequence in NCBI

First, we opened the [NCBI Protein database](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/protein/) and searched for **NEU1 Homo sapiens** to find the human protein sequence. We selected the full-length RefSeq protein record and checked its accession number to make sure we were using the correct protein. The accession number selected was **NP_000425.1**, corresponding to the sialidase-1 precursor. We also recorded the gene name, protein name, accession number, and normal function of the enzyme.

### Step 2: Preparing the R script

Next, we used the `lysosomal_enzyme_analysis.R` script supplied with the laboratory manual. The script was designed to retrieve a protein sequence from NCBI and analyze its amino acid composition and potential N-glycosylation motifs.

We placed the script in our project folder and changed the example gene name and accession number to our selected enzyme:

```r
gene <- "NEU1"
accession <- "NP_000425.1"
```

### Step 3: Installing the required packages

Before running the script, we installed the R packages required for the analysis. We used `rentrez` to retrieve the protein sequence from NCBI and `ggplot2` to create the graphs.

We used the following command in RStudio:

```r
install.packages(c("rentrez", "ggplot2"))
```

These packages need to be installed in the R environment before the script can use them.

### Step 4: Running the script

After preparing the script and installing the packages, we ran the complete code in RStudio. The script retrieved the NEU1 protein sequence from NCBI and saved it as a FASTA file.

It then calculated the total number of amino acids, counted each amino acid type, calculated their percentages, and searched for potential N-glycosylation motifs. A potential N-glycosylation motif follows the pattern N-X-S or N-X-T, where X can be any amino acid except proline (P).

### Step 5: Checking the results

Finally, we checked the output files generated by the script. The CSV files contained the protein summary, amino acid counts and percentages, and positions of potential N-glycosylation motifs. We also opened the two PNG figures to examine the amino acid composition and the locations of the potential motifs.

We used these outputs to answer the five questions in the laboratory manual.

## 8. Answers to the Laboratory Questions

### 1. What does your enzyme normally break down inside lysosomes?

NEU1 breaks down molecules such as glycoproteins and glycolipids by removing their terminal sialic acid residues. This helps with the breakdown and recycling of substances inside lysosomes.

### 2. What is the total length of your protein, and which amino acids are most common?

The NEU1 protein sequence analyzed contains **415 amino acids**. The most common amino acid is glycine (G), with 40 occurrences or 9.64%. It is followed by serine (S), with 39 occurrences or 9.40%; leucine (L), with 37 or 8.92%; valine (V), with 35 or 8.43%; and alanine (A), with 28 or 6.75%.

### 3. How many potential N-glycosylation motifs did you find? Where are they located?

The analysis identified **three potential N-glycosylation motifs**:

- Position 186: NLS
- Position 343: NLT
- Position 352: NGT

These sequences match the N-X-S/T pattern, where X is not proline. However, finding these patterns does not prove that the protein is actually glycosylated.

### 4. Why can glycosylation matter for lysosomal proteins? Does finding a motif prove the protein is glycosylated?

Glycosylation can help proteins fold correctly, maintain stability, and undergo proper processing and transport within the cell. These processes can be important for a protein to reach the correct location and function properly.

However, finding a potential N-glycosylation motif does not prove that a sugar group is attached to the protein. Our analysis examines the amino acid sequence only; it does not experimentally confirm glycosylation or the presence of a mannose-6-phosphate tag.

### 5. What could happen in the cell if your enzyme did not work properly?

If NEU1 does not function properly, sialic-acid-containing substances may accumulate in lysosomes because they are not broken down normally. Mutations that impair NEU1 can cause **sialidosis**, a lysosomal storage disease. The effects can vary depending on how much enzyme function remains.
